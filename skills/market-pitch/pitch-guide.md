# Pitch guide

The reference `market-pitch` loads every run. `market-launch` and `market-rehearse` load its Evidence section. It covers positioning, demand, buyer fear, the rules for words, and the rules for evidence. Tune it over time, because it is the spec.

## Positioning comes first

Copy written on a broken frame is wasted work. Before you draft a sentence, answer six questions out loud:

- **Competitive alternatives:** if you didn't exist, what would they use? Including a spreadsheet, an intern, and doing nothing.
- **Unique attributes:** what can you do that those alternatives cannot?
- **Value and proof:** what do those attributes let the customer do, and what proves it?
- **Target market:** who cares *disproportionately* about exactly that value?
- **Market category:** what frame makes the value obvious to those people?
- **Trend** (optional): what shift already on their radar makes this urgent now?

**Market category is a choice rather than a fact.** A product sold as "email" invites every expectation of email (a calendar, threading, filters) and gets judged on the ones it lacks. The same product framed as team collaboration is suddenly obvious. When the current frame invites expectations the product doesn't meet, change the frame before you change the words.

**The customer's view of the competitive set is the only one that counts.** Who you think you compete with is trivia. Who they'd buy instead is the whole game.

The trend layer is the easiest thing here to get wrong. Add it only when the target buyer already tracks that trend. Otherwise you're explaining two ideas at once and getting neither across.

## Always start from demand

Supply-side asks *who needs my product* and pushes features at an imagined average customer. Demand-side asks *what progress is this person trying to make, and what pushed them to look now*. Only the second one creates pull.

**The same forces decide every purchase.** The push of the situation, the magnetism of the new thing, the anxiety about the new thing, and the habit of the present. Push plus pull must outweigh anxiety plus habit, or nothing moves.

- **Cut anxiety before you add magnetism.** Adding features is the expensive way to move someone. State the three things the buyer is afraid of (*will it actually work? can I even tell these options apart? what if I pick wrong?*) and answer each one in the copy. That's cheaper and it converts better.
- **Every asset serves one buying stage.** First thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, onboarding, ongoing use. State the stage before you write, because copy aimed at "everyone" is aimed at nobody.
- **Ways to create a first thought.** Ask a good question and leave it unanswered, or tell a story. Give them a new metric, or state the obvious. Only these open space in someone's head.
- **Without a deadline, people look forever.** Find the real one in their world (a renewal, a launch, a quarter close, a person leaving). Manufactured urgency reads as manufactured.
- **Functional and emotional motivation both decide.** The emotional one never appears in a requirements doc. Buyers downsizing to a smaller home need a room for the dining table, even though they'll never host anything, because every birthday they ever had happened at that table. Find that room in your product.
- **Chain the why until the real job appears.** They need a drill because they want a hole. They want the hole for a plug and the plug for a lamp, because it's hard to see and they want to read. Stop at "drill" and you'll sell drills to someone who needed a Kindle.
- **Never accept the first answer.** There is a reason behind every purchase. "I bought it on impulse" means *how long haven't you been able to sleep?* followed by *two years.*
- **The stated reason is rarely the real reason.** People act on motives they'd rather not say out loud (status, belonging, looking competent). Write copy that serves the motive they'll admit to while quietly satisfying the one they won't. Never make them say the second one.

## The buyer is afraid

Buying is harder than selling. Buyers worry far more about *messing up* than about missing out, which makes the safest decision no decision at all.

- **The status quo is the competitor in every asset**, not just the vendors on the shortlist. Make the case for change before you make the case for you.
- **Show them how to buy.** The options that exist, the honest tradeoffs between them, and when to pick you. Not a feature walkthrough. The best thing you can be to a buyer is a knowledgeable guide who leaves them able to decide for themselves.
- **They're the expert in their pain, and you're the expert in the solutions.** Ask about the first, and teach about the second. Reversing that is how discovery calls turn into interrogations and pitches turn into brochures.
- **Only differentiated value wins.** Delete any claim a competitor could paste onto their own page unchanged. Whatever survives is your pitch.
- **Aim at the champion.** They build the shortlist and cut you before any other stakeholder ever sees your name.
- **Package the tradeoffs the buyer will accept** rather than denying there are any. Everyone gives something up to make progress, and stating the tradeoff builds more trust than hiding it.

## The words

- **Show, don't claim.** Every claim has proof beside it (a number, a named customer, a demo, a real quote). A claim you can't prove gets cut, not softened.
- **Kill generic prose.** If a competitor could publish the same sentence, delete it. Generic copy isn't a style problem. It means you haven't found what's true and specific yet.
- **Simplify rather than compress.** One clear problem-solution line first, the tradeoff map after. When a pitch feels too long, make the idea simpler, and don't squeeze the same complexity into fewer words. The simplest ideas have the largest markets.
- **Avoid sevens.** Take a side. A hedged headline is a non-answer written to avoid offending anyone, and it persuades no one.
- **Lead with the outcome rather than the tech.** State the category by the work it removes from the buyer's day, not by what's inside the box. "AI-powered" describes you, and "you stop re-reading every ticket" describes them.
- **Inverted pyramid.** Most important to the *reader* first, then descending. Never open on the state of the industry.
- **Cover the basics once**, meaning what it is, who it's for, how it works, when and where to get it, and why they should care.
- **Identity claims need a real behavior change.** You may ask a reader to become someone different (a company made impatience a virtue because its product was instant). Do it only when the product actually causes that change and only when you state the change. Otherwise it's flattery.

## Evidence

**Never invent a persona, a quote, a metric, a logo, or a case study.** Not as a placeholder, not as an illustration, not "for now." One fabricated number destroys the credibility of every real one next to it. The same rule applies to a rehearsal in `/market-rehearse`, where a simulated buyer doesn't produce evidence, only questions to put to a real one.

Gather real language from whatever sources this session has. Load the relevant connector tools via ToolSearch (support inbox, CRM, tracker, chat, docs) and pull what customers actually said, in their words. Search the repo and the web for the product's own material. If a source isn't available, ask the user for a paste.

Without evidence there is no claim. State the gap in the output and say what would close it. A stated gap is a finished deliverable, and a plausible invention is a broken one.

**Market benchmarks need a source too.** A conversion rate, a channel average, a competitor's numbers, or a "typical launch does X" figure is a claim like any other. Cite where it came from, or mark it as your own guess. Numbers about the market are the ones most often stated without a source, and they're the ones setting the thresholds you'll act on.

And prefer the honest small number. A few users who love the thing count for more than a big number nobody feels, and they're the ones a sharp reader will believe.
